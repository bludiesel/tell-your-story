>> Text layouts
> start here
## What this book is

This is the **reference lesson**. Every page demonstrates exactly one feature,
so an assistant shaping real content has a worked layout to copy rather than a
paragraph to interpret.

Read it with `CAPABILITIES.md` open beside you: that file says *what* each
feature is, this one shows *how a page using it actually looks*.

---

> plain page
## A plain page of prose

The default. A heading, a couple of paragraphs, nothing else — and it is the
right answer far more often than the fancier layouts are.

Keep a page to one idea. Around 130 words fills the space comfortably; past
about 170 the text starts crowding the outer edge. `node scripts/prep.ts` measures this
for you rather than making you count.

---

> lists
## Lists, and when to use one

Use a list when the order matters or the items are genuinely parallel. Prose
that has merely been chopped into bullets is harder to read, not easier.

- Confirm the valve is the right one
- Watch the gauge fall — do not assume
- Tag it so nobody reopens behind you

Numbered lists work the same way when sequence matters.

>> Emphasis blocks
> the four callouts
## Note, tip, warning, takeaway

Four ways to lift something out of the body copy. They are typeset **into** the
document — unlike a sticky note, which sits on top of it.

:::note Worth knowing
A note is a caveat that belongs to the text — background the reader needs but
which would interrupt the sentence.
:::

:::tip Do it this way
A tip is a shortcut or a good habit. Something that makes the job easier.
:::

---

> the loud ones
## When it actually matters

:::warning Never do this
A warning is for the cases where getting it wrong hurts someone or breaks
something. Use it sparingly — a page of warnings reads as a page of noise.
:::

:::takeaway The one thing
A takeaway is the single idea that must survive after everything else is
forgotten. One per section at most.
:::

---

> the human voice
## Sticky notes

A `:::sticky` is not a callout. It is the thing a trainer says out loud but
would not put in the manual — an aside in a human voice, stuck on afterwards.

:::sticky Remember
The gauge is the proof. Your memory isn't.
:::

Colour and tilt rotate automatically across the notes on a page, so two in a
row never look like a printed grid.

:::sticky {.with-previous}
A note with no title works too — sometimes the remark is the whole point.
:::

> pacing
## One press, one idea

Blocks arrive **one press at a time**, so you can talk to a point while it is
the only thing on the page. Any clicker drives it — the same key that turns a
page brings in the next block, and only turns once the spread is spent.

:::warning {.step-first}
Order matters more than you think. A warning that arrives after the setup has
already been ignored by the person it was written for.
:::

:::takeaway {.step-last}
Nothing reflows on the page. The content arrives in place, in the order it is
meant to be understood.
:::

Three markers bend the default, and `node scripts/prep.ts` proposes them for you:
`{.step-first}` to arrive with the page, `{.with-previous}` to share a press
with the block above, and `{.step-last}` for the line that should land after
its own argument. This page uses two of them: the warning above was written
second and arrives first, and the takeaway was written here and arrives last —
which is why you are reading this paragraph before it.

>> Diagrams
> flow
## A process, drawn

`:::diagram flow` turns a pipe-separated line into a real SVG, generated by
svg.js when the book is built. Nothing of the drawing library reaches the
reader — and because it is emitted inline rather than as an image, the
connectors draw themselves on and the boxes arrive in sequence as the page
turns.

:::diagram flow
Isolate | Prove dead | Tag it | Work
:::

---

> cycle
## Something that repeats

`:::diagram cycle` closes the loop instead of ending it. Use it when the last
step feeds the first — a review cycle, an inspection round, a shift handover.

:::diagram cycle
Plan | Do | Check | Act
:::

---

> bars
## Comparing a few numbers

`:::diagram bars` is for a handful of values where the *comparison* is the
point. The first row takes the accent colour, and the bars grow from their
baseline as the page arrives, because a bar chart reads as measurement.

:::diagram bars
Reported | 42
Investigated | 31
Closed | 27
Outstanding | 4
:::

> tables
## A table

| Question | Your answer |
|---|---|
| Which valve do you close first? | |
| How do you prove a line is dead? | |
| What do you do if you are alone? | |

An empty column makes a table something the reader fills in. Keep tables under
about a dozen rows — a printed page cannot scroll.

---

> two columns
## Side by side

:::columns
Use columns when two things are genuinely being compared, or when a picture and
its explanation belong together on one line.

Do not use them merely to fit more on a page — that is what a second page is
for.
:::
